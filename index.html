Notes for participants

Bankable projects, bond types, listing and credit ratings

1. Choosing bankable projects · 2. Types of bonds · 3. Listing a bond on the BSE · 4. Credit ratings and creditworthiness

1. Choosing bankable projects in the council environment

A project is bankable when a lender or investor can see how it will be repaid. A good idea is not enough: the project needs a paying customer, realistic costs and someone accountable for running it.

Start from the council's own position. The project must fall within the council's functions and its development plan, and the council's approvals must be planned: the full council resolution, the Minister's approval of any loan under section 67 of the Local Government Act, and procurement under the Public Procurement Act. Councils depend heavily on grants, so the repayment must come from the project's own income or from proven collections, not from the Revenue Support Grant.

Principles of a bankable project.

  1. A clear need, with evidence of demand.
  2. A paying customer and a reliable revenue stream: user charges, rentals, gate fees or an offtake agreement.
  3. Realistic capital and operating costs, and a realistic timetable.
  4. Legal authority, land and permits secured before money is raised.
  5. Risks identified and given to the party best able to manage each one.
  6. The capacity to build, operate and maintain the asset.
  7. Environmental and social soundness.
  8. Better value than the alternatives, including doing nothing.

Methods and tools.

  • Long-list and screen. List the candidate projects from the development plan, then score them on weighted criteria, for example revenue certainty 30, strategic fit 20, affordability 20, readiness 15, social and environmental benefit 15. Take the top one or two forward.
  • One-page concept note. The need, the solution, the cost, who pays, the main risks and the approvals required.
  • Pre-feasibility, then feasibility. Demand, technical design, cost, financial and economic returns, legal position, environmental and social impact, and who will run it.
  • Financial model. Yearly cash flows over the life of the loan. Net present value (NPV) is the value today of the future cash flows less the cost; a positive NPV at the cost of borrowing means the project creates value. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the rate at which the NPV is zero; it should exceed the cost of borrowing. Payback is the number of years to recover the investment.
  • Debt service cover ratio (DSCR). Cash available for debt service divided by the year's interest and principal. Lenders usually look for at least 1.3 to 1.5 times.
  • Sensitivity and stress tests. What happens if income falls by 20% or costs rise by 15%? If the DSCR falls below 1.0, the project cannot repay on its own.
  • Risk register. Each risk, its likelihood and impact, who owns it and how it is reduced.

2. Types of bonds and how councils can use them

TypeHow it worksCouncil use
General obligation bondRepaid from the council's general revenue, such as rates and fees.Roads, street lighting and other works that do not earn their own income. Needs strong own revenue.
Revenue bondRepaid only from the income of the project it pays for.Markets, bus termini, abattoirs, landfill gate fees, water and sanitation charges.
Project bondIssued by a project company (an SPV) whose cash flows are ring-fenced for investors.A solar plant with an offtake agreement; PPP projects.
Green bondProceeds go only to environmental projects, under a framework checked by an independent reviewer.Solar, recycling, water efficiency. Listed in the BSE's Sustainable Bonds Segment.
Social bondProceeds go only to social projects.Affordable housing, clinics, schools.
Sustainability bondA mix of green and social projects.Integrated neighbourhood upgrades.
Sustainability-linked bondThe coupon changes if agreed targets are met or missed.Council-wide targets, for example the collection rate.
Asset-backed bondRepaid from a pool of rentals or receivables held by an insolvency-remote SPV.Property rentals and lease income.
Pooled bondSeveral councils combine projects into one issue through a shared vehicle.Smaller councils reaching a size investors will buy.
Guaranteed or credit-enhanced bondA guarantee, a reserve account or a partial guarantee lowers the risk to investors.First-time issuers.
Medium-term note programmeOne programme document approved once; separate tranches issued as projects become ready.A pipeline of projects over several years.

Structures can also differ: fixed or floating rate; bullet (all principal at the end), amortising (principal repaid over time) or with a sinking fund; and a public listed issue or a private placement to a few investors. A listed bond needs at least five holders on the BSE.

3. Listing a bond on the Botswana Stock Exchange: the steps

  1. Authority and approvals. Full council resolution; the Minister's approval of the loan under section 67, on the advice of the Minister of Finance; the issuing company's board resolution.
  2. Appoint the team. A Sponsor is required for every debt listing and must be a BSE Registered Adviser if external. Add the arranger, legal advisers, auditors, a trustee, a paying agent and a transfer secretary, and a rating agency if a rating is sought.
  3. Prepare the disclosure documents. A programme memorandum or offering circular with a pricing supplement for each issue; audited IFRS financial statements for at least three years; full details of any security, guarantee or credit enhancement; and the rating, if one is obtained.
  4. Informal review. The Sponsor submits the drafts to the BSE for comment.
  5. Roadshow and book-building. The arranger presents to investors, who place bids; the coupon and the allocation are set from the bids.
  6. Formal approval. The BSE approves the signed final disclosure document, which reaches investors at least 48 hours before listing.
  7. Settlement and listing. The bonds are held electronically at the CSDB, admitted to the BSE's Official List and announced on X-News.
  8. After listing. Interest and capital are paid through the paying agent; financial statements are published on time; the trustee watches the covenants; any event of default is announced on X-News within one business day.

Terms to know.

  • Issuer: the body that borrows and repays, often the council's SPV or investment company.
  • Sponsor: the adviser who submits the application to the BSE and confirms the issuer meets the listing requirements.
  • Arranger: the bank or adviser who structures the bond and finds investors.
  • Trustee: acts for the bondholders and enforces their rights.
  • Paying agent: pays the interest and principal to the holders.
  • CSDB: the Central Securities Depository of Botswana, which keeps the record of who owns the bonds.
  • Programme memorandum: the main disclosure document for a programme of issues. Pricing supplement: the short document for each issue, with its amount, coupon and maturity.
  • Coupon: the interest rate. Tenor: the length of the loan. Maturity: the date the principal is repaid.
  • Book-building: collecting investor bids to set the price and allocation.
  • Covenant: a promise in the bond terms, for example to keep a minimum DSCR. Event of default: a breach, such as a missed payment, that lets holders act.
  • X-News: the BSE's announcement service. Official List: the BSE's list of listed securities.

4. Credit ratings and creditworthiness

A credit rating is an independent opinion on whether the issuer will pay interest and principal in full and on time. It is expressed as a grade from AAA, the strongest, down to D, which means default. Grades of BBB- and above are called investment grade. A national scale compares issuers within Botswana; a global scale compares them with issuers worldwide.

How a rating is done. The issuer appoints a rating agency; the agency asks for audited accounts, budgets, debt and collection records and governance documents; it meets management and may visit; a rating committee decides; the rating is published; and it is reviewed at least once a year, with an outlook of positive, stable or negative.

What analysts look at for a council. The legal and fiscal framework (powers, grants and oversight); the local economy and revenue base; financial performance, especially the operating surplus; flexibility to raise own revenue; debt and other obligations; liquidity; and management and governance, including timely audits and budget discipline.

Indicators every council can calculate now.

  • Own-revenue ratio: own revenue divided by total revenue.
  • Collection rate: cash collected divided by the amount billed in the same period (not divided by the whole debtor book).
  • Operating surplus: recurrent revenue less recurrent expenditure.
  • Debt service cover: cash available for debt service divided by the year's interest and principal.
  • Debt to revenue: total debt divided by annual revenue.
  • Liquidity: cash held divided by average daily operating spending, in days.

On the BSE, a rating is not compulsory for a debt listing, but if one is obtained it must be disclosed, and any change must be announced on X-News within 24 hours. Pension funds and other large investors expect one.
